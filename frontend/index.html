See exactly when satellites pass over your sky

Pick any city - or click any point on the map - and this site calculates real satellite passes for that spot: when they rise, how high they climb, and whether they'll actually be visible to the eye. How It Works

1

Satellites orbit at different heights and speeds

A satellite close to Earth (like the ISS) circles it in about 90 minutes; one far away can take 12 hours or more.

2

From the ground, that looks like a "pass"

As Earth turns and the satellite moves, it periodically rises above your horizon, crosses the sky, then sets - that whole event is a pass.

3

This site calculates that for any point on Earth

Using real orbital data (TLEs from Celestrak) and the same SGP4 propagation method used in real satellite tracking, for any city or any point you click.

How "Best Pass" is chosen

Among all upcoming passes, we filter down to the ones that will actually be visible to the eye - sunlit, not just geometrically above the horizon - then highlight whichever one climbs highest in the sky. The higher a pass peaks, the easier it is to spot and the longer it stays clear of horizon haze and obstructions, which is why elevation is the deciding factor.

Glossary

A field guide to the terms used throughout this app - if you're new here, or just want to know what "elevation 62°, azimuth 226°" actually means, start here.

Pass

The whole time a satellite is above your horizon - it rises, climbs to its highest point (peak), then sets. Everything in "Upcoming Passes" describes one of these, start to finish.

Elevation

How high above your horizon to look, in degrees. 0° is right at the horizon; 90° is directly overhead. "Peak elevation" is the highest point a pass reaches.

Azimuth

Which direction to face, as a compass bearing: 0°=North, 90°=East, 180°=South, 270°=West.

Altitude

How far above Earth's surface the satellite is actually orbiting, in km - not the same as elevation. A satellite can be very high in altitude while still low in elevation (near your horizon), or the reverse.

Orbit Type

The category a satellite's path falls into - LEO, MEO, GEO, or HEO - based on how high up and how fast it orbits. This is what actually determines how a satellite behaves in your sky: how often it passes over, how long each pass lasts, and whether it seems to move at all.

Visible (Sunlit)

Being above the horizon isn't the same as being visible to the eye - a satellite also needs to be lit by sunlight while your sky is dark (dawn, dusk, or night) to actually be seen. Passes and "Visible Right Now" both check for this.

Ground Track

The point on Earth's surface directly beneath a satellite at a given moment. The animated line on the map traces this - not a line pointing at the satellite in the sky, which is why it can look far from your city for high-altitude satellites.

TLE

Two-Line Element set - the compact, standardized snapshot of a satellite's orbit this entire app is built from, published by NORAD and pulled live from Celestrak. Every number on this page traces back to one of these.
