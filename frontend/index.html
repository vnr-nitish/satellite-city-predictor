      Understanding Orbits

      A quick reference for what the orbit-type labels above actually mean - useful if you're a student, a satellite-tracking hobbyist, or just curious why some passes last minutes and others last hours.

      LEO — Low Earth Orbit

      Altitude: ~160-2,000 km

      Orbital period: ~90-120 minutes

      Example here: ISS, Hubble Space Telescope

      Close and fast (~7.8 km/s) - a LEO satellite crosses your entire sky in just a few minutes, which is why these passes are short but frequent, often several times a day.

      MEO — Medium Earth Orbit

      Altitude: ~2,000-35,786 km

      Orbital period: a few hours up to ~12 hours

      Example here: GPS satellites

      High enough to move much more slowly across the sky than a LEO satellite - a single MEO pass can stay above the horizon for hours instead of minutes.

      GEO — Geostationary Orbit

      Altitude: ~35,786 km

      Orbital period: ~24 hours (matches Earth's rotation)

      Example here: GOES, Meteosat weather satellites

      Doesn't really "pass over" at all - a geostationary satellite stays fixed at the same point in the sky permanently, which is exactly why it's used for weather and broadcast satellites: a dish never has to move to track it.

      HEO — Highly Elliptical Orbit

      Altitude: varies hugely - low at perigee, very high at apogee

      Orbital period: several hours, depending on the orbit's design

      Example here: Chandra X-ray Observatory, NASA POLAR

      Speed changes constantly through the orbit - fast near perigee (closest to Earth), very slow near apogee (farthest away) - so pass duration for the same satellite can vary enormously depending on where it is in its orbit.
